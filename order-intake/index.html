01 · Problem

Every blocker became a manual investigation across systems and teams.

Multi-step

Resolution depended on repeated investigation and handoffs across systems and partner teams.

Daily triage started with files

Operations teams reviewed recurring extracts to identify demands that had not entered reservation.

The reason was fragmented

Status, exceptions, planning changes, and ownership lived in different tools and data sources.

Every blocker had a different path

Users had to know which partner team owned the issue and how that specific exception was resolved.

Progress lived in messages

Follow-ups, decisions, and verification were distributed across individual chats and email threads.

02 · Embedded research

I designed with the people doing the triage—not from a static requirements list.

The research happened inside delivery: contextual questions, workflow walkthroughs, co-design messages, live demos, blocker-by-blocker UAT, production feedback, and debugging sessions with engineering partners.

Workflow inquiry

Mapped the current triage process, ownership handoffs, recurring questions, and criteria used to decide whether a demand belonged in scope.

Information architecture

Asked users which fields belonged in the grid, which attributes needed filters, and what status categories matched their mental model.

Collaborative UAT

Compared the UI against a shared validation sheet blocker by blocker to find missing records, incorrect states, and conflicting rules.

Production learning

Used live feedback, backend logs, and collaborative debugging to refine selection behavior, filtering, eligibility, loading, and error states.

The experience needed to explain why a demand had not started reservation and what happened before it reached that state.The recurring operational question that anchored the experience.

03 · Task-flow redesign

Turn an open-ended investigation into a progressive decision path.

Before

  1. Open the daily triage file.
  2. Search multiple systems for the demand.
  3. Interpret status and exception fields.
  4. Find the blocker owner.
  5. Ask for an update through chat or email.
  6. Track progress manually and verify resolution.

After

  1. Search or filter the operational population.
  2. Read a clear readiness status.
  3. Inspect active exceptions and event history.
  4. Ask for a plain-language explanation.
  5. Switch to resolution for eligible blockers.
  6. Apply the fix and see the updated state.

04 · Design principles

Make scope, evidence, and action boundaries visible.

Use operational language

Status categories and filters reflect how planners already discuss readiness: blocked, ready, pending, completed, or missing approval.

Separate explaining from fixing

Explicability and resolution share context, but each view optimizes for a different user intent and level of risk.

Make action boundaries explicit

Only eligible rows can be selected, and already-remediated items become unavailable for repeat action.

07 · Evaluative testing

UAT exposed where the system model and the user model diverged.

Blocker-by-blocker validationRecords were present in one view but missing in another

Aligned scope criteria and distinguished explainability from resolution populations.

Status-model feedback“Blocked” and “ready” were not enough

Expanded the model to include completed, pending, and missing-approval states.

Policy changeApproval rules changed during delivery

Updated filters and status logic instead of leaving users to infer why records disappeared.

Production debuggingFiltered bulk selection sent more rows than expected

Corrected selection handling and made action scope easier to verify.

Interaction feedbackUsers lost the selected row’s details below the grid

Added automatic focus and scroll to the evidence area.

Lifecycle feedbackResolved items could invite duplicate action

Disabled completed remediation and clarified post-fix state.

10 · Reflection

Complex enterprise UX depends on making business rules inspectable.

What I learned

A clear UI cannot compensate for invisible scope rules. Users need to see why a record appears, why it is excluded, and why an action is enabled.

What I would improve

I would establish event instrumentation and task-based usability measures earlier, then compare time-to-explanation and successful-remediation rates before and after launch.

Takeaway

I turned a rules-heavy operational process into a workflow people could understand, verify, and act on.

The project demonstrates how I combine embedded research, interaction design, frontend engineering, and cross-system collaboration in high-complexity enterprise products.
