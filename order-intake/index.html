the short version

This was not a clean, linear design exercise. I was learning the workflow while building it, checking the interface against real operational data, and revising both the interaction and the implementation as planners found gaps.

The original request sounded simple: help users answer, “Why hasn’t my demand started reservation?” In practice, the answer was spread across planning data, exception records, reservation events, spreadsheets, email, and knowledge held by different teams. I turned that fragmented investigation into a product people could understand and trust—without hiding the rules or automating decisions that still needed planner judgment.

the problem

Operational teams started with a recurring spreadsheet of demands whose reservation dates were approaching or already in the past. A blocked demand then triggered a manual loop: identify the blocker, find its owner, contact the right team, follow up, verify the change, and repeat if reservation was rejected again.

What prevented this order from reserving?

Is it blocked, ready, or already reserving?

Which events actually matter?

What can I do next?

  1. Find the demand in a recurring operational extract.
  2. Look across systems for status, exception, and planning context.
  3. Determine which team owns that blocker type.
  4. Contact the owner through email or chat and wait for a response.
  5. Return later to verify whether the blocker was resolved.
  6. Start the loop again if reservation still did not proceed.

The design challenge was not simply to display more data. It was to make the current state understandable, show the evidence behind it, and shorten the distance from awareness to a safe next action.

how I learned the workflow

I did not run a separate formal research phase. Research happened inside delivery: in workflow walkthroughs, prototype reviews, UAT sessions, production discrepancies, support questions, and the messages people sent when the product did not match what they expected.

Workflow mapping

I traced how a demand moved from detection through ownership, follow-up, correction, and reservation.

Co-design conversations

I asked planners which fields, filters, status categories, and pieces of evidence matched the way they actually triaged work.

Blocker-by-blocker UAT

We compared the interface against a shared validation sheet to find missing demands, incorrect states, and conflicting rules.

Production feedback

I treated bug reports, empty states, confusing selections, and navigation issues as usability evidence—not just implementation defects.

what I heard

01 / explain the state

Users needed the reason, not another status label.

A blocked state was useful only when the relevant exception and reservation history were understandable.

02 / focus the work

Completeness could create more noise.

Users wanted a focused population and filters that reflected geography, resource, planning intent, status, and blocker category.

03 / earn trust

Data accuracy was part of the experience.

Missing demands and conflicting statuses weakened confidence faster than a visual design improvement could restore it.

04 / connect awareness to action

A notification alone did not resolve anything.

Users needed to land on the relevant order with its context intact, then act on one or several eligible demands.

design exploration

I considered the experience as a system of touchpoints rather than assuming the answer had to be a single page or an AI chat.

Spreadsheet + email

The lowest-cost direction preserved familiar habits, but it did not solve fragmented investigation, repeated questions, or limited reservation history.

Chat-first agent

Conversation worked well for questions and summaries, but it was weaker for scanning many demands, comparing rows, filtering, and completing bulk actions.

UI-first workspace

A dedicated interface supported detailed investigation and batch work, but depended on users knowing where to navigate and what to search for.

Hybrid workflow

selected direction

Notifications created awareness, an agent supported explanation, the workspace handled investigation and resolution, and deeplinks preserved context between them.

the solution

The final direction connected four moments in the workflow while keeping each interaction model focused on what it did best.

01 Detect

Identify demands that need attention.

02 Notify

Surface the issue where operational work already starts.

03 Explain

Show status, blocker evidence, and meaningful history.

04 Resolve

Act on eligible demands while retaining planner approval.

Reservation Explicability

A diagnostic view for understanding what happened and what to investigate next.

  • Ready, blocked, and missing-input quick filters
  • Exception details and supporting links
  • Relevant planning fields and demand status
  • A focused reservation-history visualization
  • Filters aligned to operational planning dimensions

Exception Resolution

A separate action-oriented view for remediating demands that met explicit eligibility rules.

  • Identification of fixable demands
  • Bulk selection constrained to visible eligible rows
  • Proposed planning-date corrections
  • Planner approval or rejection
  • Results and prior-remediation state

Why two views? Understanding and actioning were related but different intents. Separating them reduced information overload and made the higher-risk remediation path easier to guard.

human-in-the-loop automation

The team deliberately chose semi-automated remediation rather than allowing an agent to make unrestricted planning changes. The system could identify a conflict and prepare a correction, but capacity planners retained approval authority.

This decision was as much a UX choice as a technical one. The interface had to communicate eligibility, proposed changes, prior actions, and completed outcomes clearly enough for someone to make a responsible decision.

how the product evolved

The interaction model and the frontend matured together. Each stage answered a new user question or reduced a different product risk.

February

Model the workflow

Created the route, sidebar, grid, details area, and visual event history around the first set of planner questions.

Reduced risk: established the interaction model before live-data integration.

Late February

Connect operational data

Integrated reservation-horizon data, dynamic columns, filter state, and selected-demand details.

Reduced risk: tested the design against real schemas and contracts.

Early March

Add evidence and explanation

Connected exception data, status quick filters, event history, and conversational context.

Improved trust: explanations could be checked against supporting records.

Mid March

Add resolution

Created a separate resolution view, eligibility behavior, remediation results, and repeat-action prevention.

Controlled risk: informational and action-oriented tasks stayed distinct.

Before UAT

Harden core interactions

Added richer filters, chip inputs, autocomplete, status logic, and collapsible grids.

Improved control: dense operational dimensions became easier to scan.

UAT

Align the workflow

Added fixability, intent, and tag filters while aligning behavior between explanation and resolution.

Improved usability: documented findings became shipped changes.

Production

Preserve context

Corrected filtered selection, added auto-scroll and deeplinks, restored fields, and clarified empty-history states.

Improved continuity: the workflow held together across entry points and states.

how feedback changed the product

The strongest evidence in this project is not a polished final screen. It is the sequence of specific changes that came directly from use.

Observation What changed
Important controls were difficult to discover. Moved high-value quick filters and the fixable-demand control above the grid.
Users needed to isolate different planning workflows. Added filters for geography, resource type, intent, planning program, and designation.
Selecting a row did not provide enough orientation. Added focus and scrolling behavior that brought the relevant detail section into view.
The grid and detail layout left excessive whitespace. Adjusted expansion, collapse, and detail-panel behavior to preserve useful density.
Users entered from notifications and other tools. Added order deeplinks that filtered, selected, and loaded the relevant demand automatically.
Empty history looked like a system failure. Replaced generic error-shaped responses with a clear “no reservation history” state.
The timeline contained too much backend event noise. Prioritized meaningful reservation and exception events over exhaustive updates.
Planners needed to distinguish automation from manual work. Added remediation status, audit context, and explicit tracking of how an action was completed.

what UAT exposed

UAT was not a final sign-off step. It showed where the system’s internal model and the planner’s mental model did not match.

Blocker-by-blocker validation

Records appeared in one view but not another.

I helped align the scope criteria while preserving the legitimate difference between explanation and resolution populations.

Status-model feedback

“Blocked” and “ready” were not enough.

The status model expanded to represent completed, pending, and missing-approval states.

Changing policy

Approval rules changed during delivery.

I updated filters and status behavior rather than leaving users to infer why records disappeared.

Production debugging

Filtered bulk selection included more rows than expected.

Selection handling was corrected so the submitted action matched the visible, eligible population.

Interaction feedback

Users lost the selected row’s details below the grid.

I added automatic focus and scrolling to the evidence section after selection.

Lifecycle feedback

Resolved demands could invite duplicate action.

Completed remediation became unavailable and the post-fix state was made explicit.

my contribution

I worked across discovery, design, implementation, and rollout. I did not own the backend business logic or the operational process alone; I partnered closely with the engineers and domain experts who did.

Discovery and synthesis

Mapped the existing triage loop and translated planner questions, validation sheets, production feedback, and data discrepancies into product requirements.

Interaction design

Defined the two-mode information architecture, status model, filter hierarchy, details flow, reservation history, deeplink behavior, and action safeguards.

Frontend engineering

Built the routed experience, schema-driven data grids, state coordination, filters, event history, conversational context, and resolution results.

Delivery and iteration

Worked with backend partners on contracts and eligibility, facilitated UAT, shipped production fixes, and returned to users for validation after changes were live.

outcomes

The workflow launched into production and continued to evolve through operational use. It connected recurring detection and notification with an interface for explanation, reservation history, and eligible remediation.

  • One connected path Users could move from notification to the relevant demand, inspect its status and evidence, and continue into resolution without reconstructing context.
  • Operational adoption The experience became part of a recurring production workflow rather than remaining a prototype or one-time demonstration.
  • Positive UAT Planner feedback confirmed that the updated flows were clearer, and the resulting changes were deployed back into production.
  • Engineering depth Fifteen completed UI pull requests under the project title covered the initial design, implementation, UAT, and rollout period, followed by additional production refinements.

What I would not claim: The broader program identified a meaningful modeled opportunity for improving on-time reservation, but I cannot attribute that forecast directly to this interface. I treat it as business context, not proven product impact.

what I learned

Explainability depends on trust. A polished visualization does not help if users cannot reconcile it with the operational data they already rely on.

The project also changed how I think about AI experiences. Conversation was useful for questions and summaries; the structured interface was better for scanning, comparison, evidence, visualization, and bulk action. The best solution used both without forcing either interaction model to do everything.

What I would do differently

I would define instrumentation earlier: notification-to-deeplink conversion, time to first action, time to resolution, bulk actions completed, recommendations approved or rejected, and investigation time before and after launch.

I would also preserve more design artifacts as the work evolved. Much of the most valuable design happened in conversations, code, and production fixes; documenting those decisions earlier would make the process easier to evaluate later.

the takeaway

I turned a rules-heavy operational process into a workflow people could understand, verify, and act on—and I built the frontend system required to keep that workflow coherent as the data and business rules evolved.
